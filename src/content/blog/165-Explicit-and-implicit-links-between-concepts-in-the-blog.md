## Introduction
As the more recent blog posts have increasingly focused on implications and consequences rather than on concept development, it seems appropriate to distil the underlying explicit and implicit structure that connects the various concepts. The result might look as follows.
## First structure
Three basic human needs, identified by scholars such as Bruce Alexander - autonomy, belonging, and achievement - provide the conceptual starting point. Their fulfilment is measured in terms of how fully each need is experienced as being met.\
The levels of fulfilment form a progression of qualitatively different configurations of psychosocial integration: dislocation, partial dislocation, *getting by* (a concept inspired by Alexander), dignity, and happiness. These terms do not denote discrete stages, but rather distinguish different configurations of experienced need fulfilment.\
Dislocation denotes a structural experience of the absence of autonomy, belonging, and achievement. Partial dislocation refers to a condition in which some spheres of life offer higher levels of experienced psychosocial integration and others low. Getting by means that the experienced levels of fulfilment are not self-sustaining. Dignity involves the experience of exercising ethical autonomy and participating in shared ethical belonging. Happiness involves the experienced fulfilment of autonomy, belonging and achievement becoming an internal source of motivation, building on the ethical orientation expressed in dignity.\
Teachable value-based competences can help move individuals towards getting by and dignity. Solidarity is the competence to engage in ethical belonging, while honor is the competence to exercise ethical autonomy.
## Second structure
Experience also underpins our understanding. At the cognitive level, experiences shape layers of thought cycles, as described by Nick Chater. These cycles build on one another through a process of creative continuation. The outcome is a personal ontology: a selection of concepts we consider relevant and the relational structure through which we organize them.\
Autonomy is the basic human need to cultivate and articulate our own perspectives on the basis of this personal ontology.\
The experiences that shape our personal ontologies extend beyond encountering situations. They also include our interactions with information, the sequences of thought cycles through which we process it, our experienced level of psychosocial integration (including our experienced material and institutional positions), and our interactions with those with whom we experience belonging, as well as with others. Nevertheless, our personal ontologies remain limited: they are never the product of all possible experiences.\
We need to respond to situations that may be either challenging or beneficial. Our personal ontologies shape how we interpret such situations and what responses we consider possible. The more adequately they reflect the realities we encounter, the better equipped we may be to respond effectively. Yet our personal ontologies are necessarily limited, and their adequacy cannot be fully established from within. We therefore face a double challenge: we need our personal ontologies to orient our responses to reality, while recognizing that the very limitations of these ontologies may prevent us from recognizing when they need to be corrected.\
We therefore need to sustain our personal ontologies while remaining capable of correcting them. Self-maintenance preserves the relational organization through which we orient ourselves and act; self-correction modifies that organization when experiences reveal its inadequacies. Neither is sufficient on its own: without maintenance, we lose continuity; without correction, we risk perpetuating inadequacies.\
The concept of autopoiesis is applied here to refer to the ongoing process of personal self-constitution, involving both the continuation of an existing personal ontology and its potential transformation. Autopoiesis does not guarantee that either process will be successful. We may preserve an inadequate ontology or encounter experiences that could enable correction without being able to make use of them.\
Because our personal ontologies are limited by default, neither dogmatism nor relativism is a viable response to reality. We cannot establish from within our own personal ontologies that they adequately capture reality as a whole; nor can we conclude that we have no grounds for distinguishing between more and less adequate understandings of reality. We can only navigate reality through an ongoing, necessarily imperfect process of muddling through, in which we sustain our personal ontologies while remaining open to their potential correction.\
Critical thinking is one mechanism through which we assess experiences and their implications for our personal ontologies. It involves assessing experiences to identify what might be valuable for correcting or reaffirming our personal ontologies. This requires relating new experiences to previous situations, earlier encounters with information, prior thought cycles, our current experience of psychosocial integration, and our past interactions with both those with whom we experience belonging and others.\
Critical thinking may contribute to self-maintenance or self-correction, but neither outcome is predetermined. Because our capacity to assess experiences is itself shaped by our existing personal ontologies, self-correction cannot be fully secured from within. It depends in part on new experiences and on what other personal ontologies make visible.\
Perhaps we also have access to a more intuitive mechanism, one that Clive Bell calls *significant form*: the capacity to experience relational organizations more directly, without first having to articulate or analyze them conceptually.\
To remain open to what encounters with others might make visible, we need to be able to suspend our reactions to their personal ontologies long enough to assess whether they might offer valuable elements for correcting or reaffirming our own. In earlier blog posts, this capacity to withhold reaction was termed *leeway*. The interval between encountering another personal ontology and filtering it through our own was termed *ma*.\
Recognizing the potential value of others’ personal ontologies, while acknowledging their irreducibility to our own, implies that we need to create and respect the conditions that allow others to remain Others, with their distinct personal ontologies. This recognition gives rise to a sense of responsibility for the Other and enables us to see and hear one another in our irreducible distinctiveness. In this way, it provides a foundation for fulfilling our basic need for belonging.
## First and second structure
The two structures are not independent. They are connected through an iterative process in which psychosocial integration and personal ontology continuously shape one another.\
Our experienced fulfilment of autonomy, belonging, and achievement influences how we experience situations, process information, develop thought cycles, and organize concepts into a personal ontology. Our personal ontology, in turn, shapes how we interpret situations, respond to opportunities and challenges, and interact with others. These responses generate new experiences, including experiences that affect our fulfilment of the three basic human needs.\
This process can reinforce itself. When our personal ontology supports responses that sustain our psychosocial integration, the resulting experiences may reaffirm both our ontology and the conditions under which it developed. But this self-reinforcement does not necessarily indicate that our ontology adequately reflects reality. It may also preserve inadequacies and limit our openness to experiences that could challenge it.\
Conversely, experiences of dislocation or partial dislocation may affect our capacity to engage with new experiences and other personal ontologies. This can make self-correction more difficult, potentially reinforcing the conditions that contributed to the dislocation in the first place. Yet challenging experiences can also expose the limitations of an existing ontology and create opportunities for change.\
The relationship between the two structures is therefore neither necessarily self-reinforcing nor necessarily self-correcting. Its direction depends, among other things, on the experiences we encounter, the thought processes through which we interpret them, our experienced level of psychosocial integration, and the conditions under which we encounter Others.\
Leeway and ma become important at this point. They can help interrupt the immediate reproduction of existing relational organizations by allowing experiences and other personal ontologies to be encountered before they are assimilated or dismissed. Whether this leads to self-correction, self-affirmation, or neither remains open.\
Because this process has no predetermined outcome, it requires both continuity and openness to change. We need our personal ontologies to orient ourselves, yet their limitations mean that we cannot establish their adequacy fully from within. Leeway and ma help preserve the possibility that encounters with other personal ontologies may contribute to self-correction without requiring immediate resolution. In this way, the ongoing process of personal self-constitution remains open to what exceeds our existing understanding.
