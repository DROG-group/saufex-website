## Introduction
To support students from going from step one (to listen without immediate reaction or judgment) to step two (avoiding premature assimilation of an external personal ontology) - see: [blog post 171](https://saufex.eu/insights/171-Fundamental-questions-regarding-human-ontologies/), students also need instruments to try and overcome defensiveness in communication. In our book ([e-book](https://jumpshare.com/share/l9PdhpCFTOq2KePRFJgP); [pdf](https://interdemocracy.wordpress.com/interdemocracy-english-pdf/)) we propose the element “Think and talk”, based on the book *Thanks for the feedback* by Douglas Stone and Sheila Heen.
## Think and talk
From our book:\
„The “think and talk” element consists of the facilitator saying three sentences, which the group of adolescents repeat one by one. The first sentence is: “I sometimes make mistakes”. The second sentence is: “Sometimes my motivation is egoistic”. The third sentence is: “I am part of the problem”.\
After the three sentences, the facilitator should ask the adolescents who felt uneasy repeating the first sentence, asking each respondent to explain their answer. The facilitator then repeats this process for the second and third sentences. The facilitator should not react to the explanations other than with a neutral “thank you”.\
Next, the facilitator explains the meaning and purpose of these sentences, which is to open the adolescents up to (negative) feedback. Negative feedback is harder for them to hear and less effective. Therefore, this serves to broaden adolescent openness to constructive confrontation, which is needed for this group even more than for adults.\
The first sentence emphasizes the importance of accepting that anyone can make a mistake. “When a mistake is pointed out to us, our first instinct is to defend ourselves or explain it away. … Accepting the fact that you will make mistakes takes some of the pressure off. … you can be confident that people make mistakes … and that some of these people are you”.\
The second sentence emphasizes that anyone can have less than noble intentions. “When we receive negative feedback about our intentions, without exception we take exception. Pursuing a certain amount of self-interest is a requirement of being alive … It may be hard to see or hard to admit. You shouldn’t stop striving to improve, but accepting what ‘is’ can be an enormous relief”.\
The third sentence emphasizes that contributions to an existing problem are not the only thing that matters. A person should feel responsible for anything they did or did not do that contributed to a problem; however, that does not mean they should feel indifferent about any problem that emerged around them without their contribution to it. By saying “I am part of the problem,” a person declares that they feel co-responsible for solving the problem. This will be appreciated by those around the person and probably lead to their mutual support at a time when the person finds themself in crisis.\
The mechanism that underpins our reluctance to admit to making mistakes, having selfish intentions, and having a role in a problem is described by Robert Kurzban  /…/. According to Kurzban, humans are social beings with an evolutionary task: to succeed in having reproductive success. Because of this, we need to “navigate the social world” to acquire and preserve the best possible position in society for us and our offspring. This means we are “in a competitive business”, even with our friends. In this competition, communication is an instrument: “communication is obviously useful for what others think in a way that works to one’s advantage, and many modular systems in the mind seem to be designed for this purpose. Indeed, I think there is some sense in which the *part of you that feels like “you” is, more or less, designed to serve this public relations function*”. Kurzban calls this module ‘the press secretary’”. He explains: “given the competitive nature of the social world, press secretary modules should be designed to cause people to behave in a way that sends out the most positive defensible message about the person’s worth, history, and future”.\
When faced with negative feedback, the press secretary, or the part that feels as “I”, tries to defend itself. It wants to uphold “the most positive defendable message” to win in the social competition. Sometimes, however, messages and actions are indefensible or diminish our social standing. This is what the element “think and talk” aims to convey.”
