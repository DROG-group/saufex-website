## 1. Do not mistake your frame for reality.
Your professional frame is a way of making reality intelligible. It is not reality itself. What appears relevant, irrelevant, normal, exceptional, factual, problematic, or actionable already depends partly on the frame through which you are looking.
## 2. Before asking what is happening, ask what makes it appear to be happening.
The first professional question should not always be “What is this?” but sometimes “What frame makes this situation appear this way to me?” The frame may be so familiar that it normally remains invisible.
## 3. Do not assume that facts are frame-free.
Facts do not interpret themselves. Deciding which facts matter, which facts belong together, what counts as evidence, and what question a fact answers already involves framing. A professional who responds to information distortion merely by adding more facts may therefore remain entirely inside the frame that helped produce the distortion.\
Even information with a high epistemic status does not become frame-free. A fact may be accurate; data may be representative; evidence may rule out particular interpretations; and proof may establish something universally. Yet the professional still has to determine what question the information addresses, what relevance it has, what alternatives remain outside consideration, and what follows from it. Epistemic strength does not eliminate framing. Alex Edman provides a useful ladder (May contain lies, 2024). This ladder ranges from statements, to facts, to data, to evidence, and ultimately to proof. Facts are accurate statements about reality. Data consist of representative facts that are not selectively chosen. Evidence is data that are sufficiently conclusive to rule out alternative interpretations. Proof represents universal evidence: information that holds across all relevant contexts.
## 4. Notice what your frame makes invisible.
Every frame reveals some things and obscures others. Ask: What would I be unlikely to notice if my current frame were wrong? The absence of something from your analysis is not necessarily evidence that it is absent from reality.
## 5. Treat your first interpretation as an interpretation.
Do not immediately turn an observation into a diagnosis, a person into a role, or an event into a category. Hold the interpretation provisionally long enough to see whether another organization of the same reality is possible.
## 6. Distinguish the situation from your response to the situation.
Your professional role normally carries expectations about what you should do. But the fact that a situation appears to demand a particular response may itself be an effect of your frame. Before acting, create a small interval between seeing and responding. Optionally, begin by describing an event through the journalist's five W's - Who? What? When? Where? Why? - while recognizing that even such a description involves choices about what is relevant.
## 7. Let the Other remain other.
Another person's interpretation is not merely information to be assessed against your existing categories. It may embody a different organization of reality. You do not have to accept it, agree with it, or abandon your own interpretation. First allow yourself to encounter what becomes visible from that position.
## 8. Resist premature evaluation.
Do not immediately classify what is unfamiliar as wrong, irrational, irrelevant, threatening, manipulative, or false. Evaluation may eventually be necessary. But if it comes first, the unfamiliar has already been absorbed into the existing frame before it has had a chance to reveal anything.
## 9. Ask what your frame would have to change if the Other were right.
The purpose of encountering another perspective is not merely to understand why they think differently. Ask instead: What would become visible to me if their way of organizing reality contained something my frame does not? This turns encounter from an exercise in explanation into an opportunity for self-correction.
## 10. Leave reality partly unresolved.
Do not close every encounter with a definitive interpretation. A professional's task may require decisions, but the decision need not imply that reality has been completely understood. Preserve an interval in which what you have encountered can remain consequential without immediately being absorbed into your existing ontology.
## The principle behind the ten commandments
These commandments do not ask professionals to abandon expertise, facts, professional standards, or judgment. They ask them to recognize that all of these operate within a frame. The professional normally needs a frame in order to act. The problem begins when the frame becomes indistinguishable from reality. The purpose of the commandments is therefore not permanent suspension. It can be understood as a temporary suspension of the assumption that one's current frame exhausts reality: a deliberate interruption of the automatic movement from perception, through framing and role definition (see: Goffman: Frame Analysis, 1974), to evaluation and action.\
That interruption creates leeway. Leeway does not mean becoming neutral. It means becoming capable, for a moment, of encountering something that your existing ontology does not yet know how to accommodate. The professional can then return to the task with the frame restored - but potentially corrected by what became visible during the interval. In this sense, the professional's discipline is not merely: “Know your facts.” It is: “Know the frame through which your facts become facts.” And beyond that: “Know when to temporarily step outside it.”
