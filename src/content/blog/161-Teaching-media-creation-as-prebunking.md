## Learning to recognize manipulation by learning to create media
In *Foolproof: Why Misinformation Infects Our Minds and How to Build Immunity* (2023), Sander van der Linden makes the case for prebunking: preparing people to recognize and resist manipulated information before they encounter it. Rather than correcting a false claim after it has influenced someone's thinking, prebunking familiarizes people with the techniques used to influence them. The idea is to develop resistance not merely to particular false claims, but to the methods through which such claims acquire their persuasive force.\
For individuals working in media literacy, this raises an educational question: could teaching students to create media be a form of prebunking?
## From recognizing manipulation to experiencing its construction
One way to prebunk misinformation is to explain manipulative techniques, demonstrate how they work, and allow learners to practice recognizing them. Van der Linden and his collaborators have also explored a more experiential approach. The Bad News game co-created by us in Drog, for example, lets participants experiment with techniques used to spread disinformation. By taking the producer's perspective, participants can learn to recognize such techniques when they subsequently encounter them.\
Media creation offers a related educational opportunity. When students produce an blog post, photograph, video, podcast or social-media post, they must make choices. They select what to include and exclude, determine what deserves attention, choose a perspective, arrange information, and decide how to address an audience. These choices are not merely technical. They help determine what a recipient will perceive and how that recipient might interpret it.\
Students who make media encounter the options to try and steer perceptions from the inside. They discover that the meaning received by an audience is not simply contained in the facts presented. It is also affected by the way those facts are selected, organized and communicated. Learning to create media can therefore provide experience of the mechanisms through which media messages may become persuasive.
## Why this experience may have preventive value
The preventive value lies in the possibility of transferring that experience to the interpretation of media encountered later. Consider a student who creates a short video about a disagreement. The student must decide how to implement basic film grammar (e.g. frame size, lighting), which information to retain, which reactions to show, where to cut, and whose perspective to foreground. Even without inventing anything, the student can produce a version that makes one participant look substantially better than another.\
If the student subsequently encounters a similarly constructed video online, the production experience may provide a useful point of reference. Instead of responding exclusively to the impression the video creates, the student may also consider the choices that could have produced that impression.\
The same applies to emotional appeals, selective presentation, false dilemmas and the construction of group identities. Creating media can make these techniques less mysterious by revealing some of the grammar and decisions through which they operate. This resembles prebunking in an important respect: the learner encounters the mechanism before encountering its potentially manipulative application in an unfamiliar context.\
The experience does not guarantee that the student will recognize manipulation. Nor does it establish that every media-production activity produces a lasting preventive effect. It provides a plausible educational mechanism whose effectiveness can be tested.
## Creation is not enough
There is, however, an important qualification. Students can learn to operate a camera, edit a video or write an engaging headline without becoming more alert to manipulation. They might even become more effective at producing manipulative media themselves. The educational value therefore depends not only on teaching media creation but also on how media creation is organized.\
Production needs to be accompanied by reflection on the relationship between choices and their possible effects. It can also be useful to compare alternative versions of the same material. When students see how different selections, sequences or framings change the impression produced, they can examine the relationship between construction and interpretation rather than simply being told that media can be misleading.\
The objective is not to teach students that media messages are inherently untrustworthy. That would replace naïve acceptance with indiscriminate suspicion. The objective is to help them recognize that media messages are constructed, that construction involves choices, and that those choices merit attention. This distinction matters because prebunking should equip learners to assess messages, not instruct them to reject messages in advance.
## Beyond the detection of falsehoods
The argument also has implications for what we understand by media literacy. If media literacy is understood primarily as the ability to distinguish true from false information, its educational emphasis will tend to fall on verification: checking claims, consulting sources, and identifying factual errors.\
These are necessary skills, but they do not address every way in which communication can distort understanding. A message may contain no demonstrably false statement and still produce a misleading impression. The problem may lie in its framing, omissions, emotional cues or the relationship it establishes between otherwise accurate pieces of information.\
Teaching media creation can direct attention toward these dimensions because students experience how they participate in constructing meaning. It also introduces a useful symmetry. Students are not merely recipients who must learn to defend themselves against the work of others. They are producers whose own choices affect what others can see and understand. This makes media literacy a question of responsibility as well as competence.
## An educational hypothesis worth testing
The argument can be stated as a hypothesis: Teaching media creation, when combined with structured reflection on selection, framing, omission and persuasive technique, can function as a form of prebunking by giving learners prior experience of how media messages are constructed and how those constructions influence interpretation.
## Step-by-step
The first step, it seems to us, is to teach students the basics of media creation and then explore the relationship between the choices these techniques make possible and the meanings they convey. Students could experiment with different formats, lighting, camera angles, shot lengths, editing, sound, and sequencing, and examine how each affects the impression produced. They can then compare alternative versions of the same material and reflect on how these choices influence what an audience sees, feels and concludes.\
The next step is to connect this experience to the media students encounter in everyday life. When they recognize the choices available to a media creator, they may become more attentive to the choices underlying the messages they receive. What has been included or excluded? Why might this particular angle, sequence or sound have been chosen? What other impression could the same material have produced?
## Understanding
This does not require students to assume that every choice is manipulative. Media creation necessarily involves selection and construction; manipulation is one possible use of these processes, not their inevitable result. The educational objective is to make the construction of media messages more visible, so that students can assess their meaning and effects rather than respond to them unreflectively.\
Whether this approach produces a measurable and lasting prebunking effect remains an empirical question. But it offers a clear proposition for media education: students who have experienced how media choices shape interpretation may be better equipped to recognize the same mechanisms when they encounter media produced by others.
