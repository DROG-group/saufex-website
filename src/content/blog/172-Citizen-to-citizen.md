## Introduction
[Blog post 171](https://saufex.eu/insights/171-Fundamental-questions-regarding-human-ontologies/) identified two steps to understand external ontologies. The basic Interdemocracy structure facilitates step one: to listen without immediate reaction or judgment. But step one does not automatically evoke step two: avoiding premature assimilation of an external personal ontology A separate didactic mechanism is needed.
## Citizen-to-citizen
In our recent book *Interdemocracy* ([e-book](https://jumpshare.com/share/l9PdhpCFTOq2KePRFJgP); [pdf]( https://interdemocracy.wordpress.com/interdemocracy-english-pdf/)) Beata Staszyńska-Hansen and I provide such a mechanism: citizen-to-citizen. From the book:\
„In essence, a citizen-to-citizen dialogue involves adolescents conducting a 90-second dialogue with each other. In this format, three people participate in the dialogue: the adolescent asking the questions, the adolescent answering the questions, and the facilitator monitoring the time. All adolescents who are not actively filling one of those roles should refrain from reacting. /…/ the participants start with a question formulated by the facilitator that is linked to the introduction. This question is the central theme of the dialogue; the adolescent asking question can do so as is or in a more personalized form.\
The 90-second dialogue is divided into three parts: the greeting and posing of the question; a dialogue in which one adolescent asks questions and one responds; and a ten-second conclusion in which the adolescent asking questions concludes the dialogue and thanks their counterpart. The task of the facilitator as the timekeeper is to signal when ten seconds are left—for instance, by shaping the letter “T” with their hands. The sign should only be seen by the adolescent that is asking questions—not the one who is responding.\
After exactly 90 seconds, the facilitator should stop the dialogue and ask the group to give the participants a round of applause. Optionally, the facilitator may present their observations of the verbal and non-verbal communication of both participants.\
/.../ all adolescents must be involved and randomly paired. Ideally, all pairs perform twice so that each adolescent can ask questions and respond.\
Before [inviting] the first pair of participants /…/, the facilitator should present guidelines for the dialogue: greet each other at the start and thank one another at the end; listen actively; be curious; use a moderate tone; keep the dialogue on topic; refrain from judgment; and show respect, patience, and delicacy. The adolescent responding should be instructed to use only the “I” form without a claim to external validation and base their answers on their own experiences.”
