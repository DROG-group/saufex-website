## Introduction
Interdemocracy seeks to strengthen the capacity for self-correction without prescribing what people or institutions should discover or conclude. Its practical interventions create opportunities for individuals to encounter perspectives that challenge their existing understanding and for institutions to learn from knowledge and experiences they might otherwise overlook. But creating these opportunities does not guarantee that they will lead to self-correction. Individuals must decide what to make of their experiences, while institutions must take responsibility for how they consider and respond to the contributions they receive. The challenge is therefore not only to design interventions that preserve the autonomy of the self-correction process, but also to ensure that institutions accept their ethical responsibility for what happens after individuals have contributed.
## The tension
The practical interventions described in the blog posts approach this challenge from different directions. Interdemocracy structures how people formulate and encounter perspectives. Youth Resilience Councils create a route through which young people's perspectives can reach institutions. Resilience Councils seek to connect relatively autonomous sources of knowledge and, ideally, citizen experience with institutional decision-making. Their common concern is to make perspectives available that might otherwise remain unseen or unheard, without determining in advance what should follow from encountering them.\
Interdemocracy, for example, protects the independent formulation of perspectives and creates an interval before immediate evaluation. Yet a procedure cannot ensure that participants genuinely encounter what another person makes visible to them. Silence does not guarantee attention, just as exposure does not guarantee understanding. The intervention can protect the opportunity for encounter, but it cannot prescribe what that encounter should reveal or whether it should lead to a revision of one's personal ontology.\
The same tension arises when perspectives reach institutions. Youth Resilience Councils can enable young people to formulate recommendations and bring them to the attention of relevant institutions. Resilience Councils seek to expose institutional understanding to knowledge and, ideally, experiences that existing arrangements may overlook. Neither arrangement guarantees that institutions will reconsider their assumptions. Nor does responsiveness require accepting every contribution. It requires making substantive consideration possible, including the possibility that a contribution reveals something the institution had not previously recognized.\
The interventions can therefore shape the conditions under which self-correction becomes possible, but they cannot complete the process or guarantee its outcome. What happens next depends on the autonomy and responsibilities of the actors involved.
## The other part
For individuals, an intervention is an invitation to an experience that may enhance their psychosocial integration within a particular group. It may strengthen their sense of autonomy, belonging, or achievement among the people with whom they interact directly. Depending in part on the institutional response, the same experience may also charge or drain their resilience batteries on a broader scale.\
Individuals retain the autonomy to use these experiences to self-correct or maintain their personal ontologies. They may revise their understanding, or they may conclude that their existing understanding remains adequate. Neither outcome can be prescribed in advance. The intervention creates an opportunity; individuals remain responsible for what they make of it. Their decision to self-correct or self-maintain does not, in itself, determine how other participants experience autonomy and belonging, although it may affect their sense of achievement.\
Institutions occupy a different position. Their responses affect not only their own capacity for self-correction and self-maintenance but also the individuals whose contributions they receive. An institution may take a contribution seriously, explain why it does not warrant a change, revise its understanding, or simply disregard it. These responses have different implications for institutional learning and for the people who have invested effort and trust in contributing.\
This creates an asymmetry of responsibility within the institutional arrangements through which the interventions operate. Individuals can decide how to interpret their experiences, but they cannot determine whether their contributions will receive serious consideration or have consequences within an institution. Institutions exercise considerable control over that part of the process. They therefore have a responsibility not only to consider what contributions reveal about themselves, but also to consider how their responses affect the people who contributed. Yet this second responsibility often remains insufficiently visible in institutional decision-making.
## The missing piece
For the interventions to work as intended, institutions must accept an ethical responsibility for their responsiveness, both in the quality of their responses and in the extent to which they respond to the contributions they receive. This requires challenging a sometimes undemocratic-liberal bias: prioritizing procedural compliance and institutional self-maintenance over taking individuals and their representations seriously enough to allow them to challenge institutional understanding.\
Institutional responses - or the failure to respond - can affect individuals' resilience batteries, charging or depleting their capacity to engage. Institutions should therefore make these consequences visible and establish safeguards against allowing internal priorities to displace responsibility toward contributors. Responsiveness cannot be left entirely to institutional discretion. It needs to be embedded in prioritized procedures that require contributions to receive substantive consideration and make the institution accountable for how it responds.\
The underlying principle follows from the limitations of institutional as well as personal ontologies. Just as individuals cannot fully assess or correct their own ontologies from within and therefore have an ethical responsibility toward the Other, institutions cannot fully recognize or correct the limitations of their own understanding from within their established frameworks. Their epistemic limitations, combined with their power to affect others, create an ethical responsibility to take those Others seriously.\
Institutional self-correction therefore requires more than openness to external perspectives. It requires responsibility for what happens to those perspectives once they have been offered. This responsibility cannot be reduced to collecting input, following consultation procedures, or issuing a formal response. It must extend to the seriousness with which contributions are considered and the accountability of the institution for its decisions.\
The next piece of the puzzle is to implement procedures that safeguard this ethical responsibility. The challenge is to make institutional responsiveness a prioritized obligation without turning it into another procedural formality - one that institutions can satisfy while continuing to maintain the very limitations that the interventions were intended to expose. Such procedures bring the meaningful implementation of democratization and decentralization substantially closer.
