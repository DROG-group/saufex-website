## Introduction
Throughout the blog posts, an argument develops that supports and helps legitimize a key SAUFEX objective: to democratize and decentralize decision-making regarding information interference.\
The argument does not appear in a single post. It emerges gradually as different problems are examined, concepts are developed, and earlier ideas are applied to new situations. The purpose of this blog post is to trace that development.
## The problem
Information interference creates a difficult problem for democratic societies. They need to detect and respond to harmful information manipulation, but they should do so without reproducing the concentration of informational power that makes such interference possible in the first place.
## The non-mirroring challenge
Powerful technology companies, media organizations, and hostile foreign powers can concentrate informational power. Governmental institutions could respond in a similar way by concentrating the authority to determine what information is trustworthy, what constitutes interference, and how society should respond.\
This would create a major risk. Concentrated epistemic power can become increasingly insulated from alternative perspectives. Institutions that need to maintain their own continuity may gradually reinforce their existing interpretations. Their understanding of reality can become increasingly self-confirming while losing contact with parts of the social reality it is supposed to understand.\
The problem is consequently not only that information interference can distort society's understanding of reality. The institutions responding to it can themselves become vulnerable to epistemic closure. If their interpretations become sufficiently concentrated and insulated from alternative perspectives, their capacity to recognize and correct their own mistakes may diminish.\
The question then becomes how a society can respond to information interference without creating another concentration of epistemic authority in the process.
## Fighting concentration with concentration
If powerful technology companies, media organizations, and hostile foreign powers can manipulate information at scale, governments may appear to need equally powerful institutions to counter them. But this does not solve the underlying problem.\
A concentrated institution remains an organization with a particular position from which it observes and interprets reality. Its experts may possess considerable knowledge, but they do not possess every relevant perspective. The institution itself is therefore epistemically limited.\
This applies to apparently opposing political responses. Technocratic or meritocratic elites may seek to protect society by concentrating epistemic authority among experts. Populist or illiberal approaches may instead concentrate it in the name of “the people” or their representatives. Despite their differences, both can reproduce the same structural problem: an interpretive system becomes increasingly insulated from alternative sources of correction.\
The issue is therefore not simply who should possess epistemic authority. It is whether epistemic authority should become concentrated at all.
## The need for autonomous sources of difference
Any organization of reality is necessarily incomplete. This applies not only to individuals but also to institutions. An institution may possess information, expertise, procedures, models, and accumulated experience, yet it cannot establish from within its own organization of reality that it contains everything relevant to understanding the world. Self-correction therefore requires access to something that lies outside the organization that is being corrected.\
For an institution, this can come from new experiences, but it can also come from other people and organizations that constitute reality differently. Their perspectives may be wrong. They may contain biases or misconceptions. But they may also make something visible that the institution itself cannot see. The epistemic value of another perspective therefore does not depend on assuming that the Other is right. It depends on preserving the possibility that the Other may contain a difference capable of revealing a limitation in one's own organization of reality.\
Decentralization is therefore not merely a way of distributing administrative responsibilities. It can be an epistemic condition for self-correction. If relevant knowledge and perspectives remain distributed among autonomous actors, a decision-making system remains exposed to differences that cannot be completely generated from within itself as a closed system. If those perspectives are absorbed into a single institutional frame before they become consequential, however, their independence is lost. What appears to be plurality may then be little more than multiple inputs into the same interpretive system.
## Why expert participation is not enough
The creation of Resilience Councils is a step in this direction. Bringing NGOs, academics, media professionals, and other non-state experts into FIMI analysis decentralizes part of the process and introduces perspectives that would otherwise remain outside government institutions. But the logic developed in the blog posts also exposes a limitation of this approach.\
If decentralization merely means distributing decision-making among a larger number of experts, the epistemic field remains narrower than society itself. The general public remains outside the process even though citizens experience the social consequences of information interference directly. This matters because information interference-related decision-making is not only a question of identifying whether a particular information claim is factually correct. It also involves deciding what a response should be.\
Fact-speaking is therefore necessary but insufficient. A factual description can establish what has happened. It cannot by itself determine what response will be appropriate, proportionate, or workable in the social environment in which it is introduced. Nor can it fully reveal what an intervention means to the people who are expected to live with its consequences. This is why earlier blog posts distinguish between fact-speaking and belief-speaking. Experiences, intuitions, interpretations, and perceptions are not necessarily substitutes for evidence. They provide another kind of information. A policy can be evidence-informed and still fail because it does not correspond to the realities in which people have to implement, experience, and sustain it.
## The citizen as an epistemic source
The argument for including citizens therefore goes beyond democratic legitimacy. Citizens are not merely the recipients of decisions made by experts. They possess situated knowledge that may not be accessible to the institutions making those decisions. They experience local social dynamics, interpret messages through particular cultural and relational contexts, and encounter consequences that may remain invisible from an institutional distance.\
This is one reason why the blog posts turn to James Surowiecki's concept of the Wisdom of Crowds. A crowd can outperform even knowledgeable individuals when its members contribute sufficiently diverse and independent perspectives and draw upon decentralized, local knowledge. The important point is not that crowds are automatically wise. They are not. The point is that collective intelligence depends upon conditions that concentrated decision-making tends to undermine: diversity of perspectives, independence in forming those perspectives, and decentralization of knowledge.\
The same conditions matter for information interference-related decision-making. If citizens are brought into the process merely to confirm conclusions already reached by experts, the epistemic value of their participation is largely lost. If their perspectives are immediately filtered through an existing institutional frame, the institution may gain additional information without gaining genuine access to another organization of reality. Democratization therefore has an epistemic dimension. It can enlarge the range of autonomous perspectives to which decision-making remains exposed.
## The danger of paternalism
When information interference is framed primarily as a threat to vulnerable citizens, it becomes tempting to treat citizens as people who need to be protected by those who know better. This can turn resilience into a paternalistic project. The problem is not that protection is never necessary. The problem is that treating citizens primarily as vulnerable recipients can remove the autonomy that democratic resilience is supposed to preserve.\
A society cannot strengthen its capacity for self-correction by systematically assuming that its members are incapable of contributing to that correction. This is why blog posts criticize both paternalistic technocracy and populist concentration. Neither provides a satisfactory answer to the underlying epistemic problem. One concentrates authority among experts; the other concentrates it in the name of a supposedly unified people.\
The alternative is not to assume that every perspective is equally valid. It is to preserve the conditions under which different perspectives can remain autonomous long enough to become consequential for one another.
## From decentralization to democratization
The argument for decentralization therefore becomes an argument for democratization as well. If an institution's capacity for self-correction depends upon continued access to autonomous sources of difference, those sources cannot be restricted to the people already inside the institutional system. They are distributed throughout society. And if citizens are themselves autonomous organizations of reality, their perspectives should not merely be collected as data for decisions made elsewhere. They need a route through which their perspectives can become consequential for collective decision-making. This is where the democratic dimension becomes necessary.\
Democratization, in this sense, is not simply a matter of giving citizens a voice because participation is intrinsically desirable. It serves a deeper function. It helps preserve a plurality of autonomous sources of perspective from which collective decision-making can be corrected. The objective is therefore not to replace experts with citizens, nor to replace institutional knowledge with popular opinion. It is to prevent any one organization of reality from becoming sufficiently dominant that it can no longer be meaningfully corrected from outside itself.
## Preserving plurality
The argument ultimately leads to a broader understanding of democracy. If every person and institution constitutes reality through an organization that is necessarily incomplete, collective self-correction requires more than accurate information. It requires continued plurality.\
Plurality has epistemic value only when the differences remain sufficiently autonomous to become consequential for one another. A collection of perspectives that has already been assimilated into one dominant framework may look plural while functioning as a single organization of reality.\
Democracy does not follow automatically from the acceptance of otherness or epistemic incompleteness. Nor does Interdemocracy claim that the current democratic system is the only possible institutional response. Within Interdemocracy, however, democracy can be understood as a solution to a specific problem: preserving a plurality of autonomous sources of otherness.\
If democratic society is to remain collectively capable of self-correction, it must preserve multiple autonomous sources of perspective rather than allowing one organization of reality to become epistemically dominant. This is why decision-making regarding information interference should be democratized and decentralized. The purpose is not simply to distribute power more widely. It is to preserve the conditions under which the institutions responding to information interference can themselves remain open to correction.\
A society that concentrates the authority to interpret reality may be able to act decisively. But if the organization making those decisions becomes insulated from autonomous sources of difference, its ability to recognize its own errors may diminish. Decentralization keeps those sources distributed. Democratization keeps them consequential. Together, they help ensure that the response to information interference does not reproduce the very epistemic concentration that makes information interference such a threat in the first place.
