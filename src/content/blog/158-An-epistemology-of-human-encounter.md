## Introduction
If a person constitutes reality through a distinctive relational organization of concepts, then every human being encounters the world through an organization that makes orientation and action possible while remaining necessarily incomplete. No person can know all the concepts and relations that might be relevant to reality, nor can a person determine entirely from within their own organization whether those relations are correct. Human encounter therefore has an epistemic significance that goes beyond the exchange of information: another person may embody an organization of reality that makes something visible that one's own organization cannot access on its own.\
This does not give the Other epistemic priority. Another person's perspective may be wrong, just as my own may be wrong. The epistemic problem of human encounter is therefore not simply how to determine whether another person's statement is right or wrong. It is how to encounter the organization of reality from which that statement arises without eliminating its difference before discovering what that difference might make visible. This leads to the following.\
## Recognize reciprocal incompleteness
The incompleteness that makes the Other epistemically relevant also applies to myself. The encounter is therefore not an opposition between a knower and someone who merely has to be understood. Both persons constitute reality from within necessarily limited organizations, and each may consequently possess something the other lacks. Neither can assume that their own organization exhausts what is relevant to reality. The epistemic value of encounter lies precisely in this reciprocal incompleteness.
## Preserve the Other's autonomy as an epistemic condition
If I immediately try to make the Other think as I do, interpret their experience for them, or replace their formulation with my own, I may produce agreement while destroying the independent source of difference from which I might have corrected my own organization of reality. Accepting another person's autonomy is therefore not only an ethical consideration; it is also an epistemic condition.\
The point is not that the Other must remain autonomous because autonomy makes their perspective correct. It is that the Other must remain capable of constituting and articulating their own organization of reality if that organization is to remain available as a possible source of correction. If the Other must become like me before I can accept what they say, the encounter can no longer provide the kind of difference from which self-correction might arise.
## Preserve an interval before evaluation
Because both persons encounter one another through necessarily limited organizations of reality, an encounter can be terminated before this difference has become accessible. Human encounters are easily converted into immediate reactions of like or dislike, agreement or disagreement, attraction or rejection, trust or distrust. Such reactions may sometimes be appropriate, but they can also determine the meaning of what the Other has expressed before the Other has become sufficiently visible.\
Leeway creates an interval in which one's own organization of reality does not immediately determine the meaning of what the Other has expressed. Within that interval, ma can become possible: another organization of reality can be encountered without immediately being absorbed into one's own. This does not require suspending judgment indefinitely. It requires postponing judgment long enough for there to be something sufficiently understood to judge.
## Do not reduce the Other to information
What another person communicates is not necessarily reducible to propositions that can be extracted and evaluated. A person's expression may reveal how that person connects experiences, concepts, distinctions, memories, values, and expectations. Reducing the contribution immediately to information therefore risks removing the very organization that makes the Other epistemically different.\
The question is consequently not only what information the Other possesses, but what the Other's way of organizing reality makes visible that my own organization may not. The epistemic value of encounter lies partly in the possibility that what is expressed cannot be fully understood by treating it merely as another item to be added to what I already know.
## Preserve the possibility of difference
For the Other to remain available as a possible source of correction, an unfamiliar expression cannot be assimilated immediately into categories already available within my own organization of reality. An unfamiliar statement may be mistaken, but it may also express a distinction, relation, experience, or framing that my own organization does not contain.\
The first task is therefore not to agree with the Other, nor to assume that the Other is right, but to preserve the possibility that there is something to encounter that my existing organization does not yet make visible.
## Do not equate unfamiliarity with error
What is unfamiliar may be wrong, but unfamiliarity itself provides no evidence of wrongness. When something does not fit an existing organization of reality, the resulting discomfort can be resolved by classifying it as irrational, irrelevant, naïve, extreme, offensive, or simply wrong. Sometimes such classifications are justified. But they can also restore familiarity before the unfamiliar has been understood.\
The epistemic task is therefore not to preserve every unfamiliar perspective. It is to avoid making unfamiliarity itself the reason for rejection. The difference must first remain sufficiently accessible for its status to become discernible.
## Distinguish incomprehension from disagreement
This makes it important to distinguish between failing to understand the Other and disagreeing with what the Other means. When another person says something that appears wrong, I may understand what they mean and disagree with it. But I may also have interpreted their statement through categories of my own without yet understanding what they mean. These are different epistemic situations.\
If the Other has first been translated into a form that makes sense only within my own organization of reality, I may be evaluating my reconstruction rather than the Other's perspective. What appears to be disagreement may therefore indicate that the organization from which the statement arises has not yet become sufficiently accessible.
## Ask questions that expose rather than constrain
Questions can help make an Other's organization of reality visible, but they can also force that organization into the categories of the person asking. A question about what the Other means can create space for a distinction that was not previously visible, while a question framed entirely in terms of the questioner's assumptions can constrain the answer to what those assumptions already make intelligible.\
The epistemically productive question is therefore not necessarily the most efficient question. It is the question that gives the Other sufficient room to articulate an organization of reality that may differ from the questioner's own.
## Do not make the Other disappear through interpretation
The Other can disappear without ever being explicitly rejected. I may appear to listen while already constructing an explanation of what the Other „really” means, summarizing the perspective before the Other has finished speaking, diagnosing it, translating it into familiar categories, or formulating a more coherent version of what I believe the Other intended.\
These actions may appear helpful, but they can replace the Other's organization of reality with my own interpretation of it. The Other then becomes an object of interpretation rather than a possible source of an organization of reality that exceeds my own. Sometimes listening therefore requires resisting the temptation to improve what the Other has said, because improving it according to my own organization may remove precisely the difference from which I could have learned.
## Keep one's interpretation corrigible
If the Other may contain something my own organization does not, then my interpretation of the Other must itself remain open to correction. I may think I understand what someone means and nevertheless have reconstructed their perspective incorrectly. The Other must therefore remain able to say that my interpretation is not what they meant.\
This is more than a conversational courtesy. It preserves the possibility that the Other can correct not only a conclusion I have reached about their statement, but also the organization through which I have interpreted the statement in the first place.
## Do not confuse agreement with correction
The possibility of correction also means that agreement cannot itself be treated as evidence that correction has occurred. If another person changes their position, they may have been persuaded, pressured, accommodated, intimidated, or simply adapted to the dominant framing. Conversely, disagreement does not necessarily mean that self-correction has failed.\
The purpose of encounter is not to produce agreement. It is to make possible a relation in which each organization of reality can become sufficiently accessible to the other that either may discover something it could not see from within its own organization.
## Treat the Other as a possible source of correction
Once the Other is understood as another autonomous and necessarily incomplete organization of reality, the epistemic significance of encounter becomes clearer. Another person may become a source of something my own organization does not contain. This gives the Other epistemic significance without giving the Other epistemic authority.\
The Other may be wrong, but the Other may also see something that I cannot see. I cannot know in advance which of these possibilities I am encountering. That is precisely why preserving the Other as a difference matters: eliminating the difference in advance would also eliminate the possibility of discovering what it contains.
## Keep correction subordinate to encounter
Correction is a possible consequence of encounter, not its prerequisite. If I enter an encounter primarily to correct the Other, I have already assigned the Other the role of someone whose deficiency I am there to repair. But if the Other is an autonomous and necessarily incomplete organization of reality, I cannot know in advance which of us is in a position to correct the other.\
The first task is therefore to make difference accessible. Only then can it become possible to determine whether that difference represents an error, a misunderstanding, an alternative organization of reality, or something that my own organization does not yet contain. Correction remains possible precisely because it has not been imposed before the encounter has been sufficiently understood.
## Preserve consequential plurality
The same problem arises when more than two people encounter one another. If several people offer different organizations of reality, converting them immediately into a common position can produce apparent agreement at the cost of epistemic diversity. A group may become more coherent while becoming less capable of correction if the differences that could have revealed its limitations are removed in the process of producing consensus.\
Plurality therefore matters not merely because different people have different opinions, but because independently constituted differences can reveal limitations in one another. For plurality to perform this epistemic function, however, the differences must remain sufficiently distinct to become consequential for one another.
## The governing principle
If every human being is an autonomous but necessarily incomplete organization of reality, then encountering another human being should preserve the possibility that the Other makes something visible that one's own organization cannot. The objective is neither to trust the Other uncritically nor to treat every perspective as equally valid. It is to preserve the epistemic conditions under which an autonomous Other can become a genuine source of difference, information, and possible correction.
The problem of human encounter is therefore not simply how to understand another person. It is how to understand another person without eliminating the difference from which one's own understanding might have to learn.
