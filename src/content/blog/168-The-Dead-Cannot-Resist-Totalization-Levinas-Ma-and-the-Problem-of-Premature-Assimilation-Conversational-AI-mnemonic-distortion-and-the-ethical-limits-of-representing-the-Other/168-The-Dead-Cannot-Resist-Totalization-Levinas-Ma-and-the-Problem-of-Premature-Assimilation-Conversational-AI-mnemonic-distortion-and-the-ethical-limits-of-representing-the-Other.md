## Introduction
Recent developments in conversational artificial intelligence have made it possible to construct interactive representations of deceased individuals. These systems, referred to by [Helena Ward (2026)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-026-01229-8) as Interactive Personality Constructs of the Dead (IPCDs), use AI to simulate the conversational responses, personality and perspectives of people who have died. They offer new possibilities for memorialisation, allowing individuals to revisit familiar interactions, explore memories and experience a form of continued connectedness with the deceased.\
However, Ward's paper, Conversational AIs and Mnemonic Distortion, identifies an important epistemic risk associated with these technologies. IPCDs can generate representations that diverge from the actual perspectives of the deceased. Such representations may subsequently become incorporated into users' memories, reshaping their understanding of the person they intended to remember. Ward describes this phenomenon as mnemonic distortion: the incorporation of generated misrepresentations into one's memory of another individual.\
This problem can be examined through two philosophical perspectives that, although distinct in their theoretical foundations, converge on the limitations and responsibilities involved in representing another person.\
The first is Emmanuel Levinas' ethical resistance to totalization: the reduction of the Other to categories and representations belonging to the subject's own understanding. The second is the Saufex blog corpus' concern with premature assimilation: the process through which an encountered experience or perspective is incorporated into an existing personal ontology before its difference has become sufficiently accessible to enable possible self-correction.\
The case of deceased individuals introduces a particular difficulty into this relationship. A deceased person can no longer autonomously articulate, clarify, or correct their personal ontology. Their preserved expressions may continue to inform and challenge the understanding of those who remember them, but those expressions are no longer an independently articulating personal ontology. They can only acquire meaning through the ontology of the person interpreting them.\
Conversational AI adds another layer. It can generate new statements that appear to extend the deceased person's participation in a relationship. Yet the resulting representation is neither the deceased person nor an autonomous continuation of their personal ontology. It is an artificial construction that can, as an experience, confrontthe living person's ontology without itself constituting another personal ontology.\
The central argument developed here is that the inability of the deceased to participate autonomously in the interpretation of their own representations increases the ethical responsibility of the living to preserve the distinction between the person and the representations through which they remember and reconstruct that person. Extending this argument beyond the deceased, the same responsibility arises whenever individuals have limited or no capacity to challenge or correct the representations made of them, such as absent or represented individuals.

## 1. Levinas: the irreducibility of the Other
A central concern in Levinas' philosophy is the relationship between the subject's understanding of reality and the irreducibility of the Other. Human beings inevitably encounter others through their own conceptual and interpretative frameworks. We attribute intentions, recognize characteristics, establish similarities and differences, and organize our experiences into meaningful representations. Such interpretation is unavoidable. The ethical problem arises when the representation is treated as equivalent to the person represented.\
Levinas challenges the tendency to understand the Other exclusively through the categories available to the Same. The Other is not simply an object of knowledge that can eventually be comprehended in its entirety. The Other exceeds the subject's conceptual grasp and thereby challenges the subject's claim to epistemic and interpretative sovereignty. This irreducibility has an ethical significance. The subject cannot legitimately treat its own understanding of the Other as exhaustive. The ethical relation therefore places a responsibility on the subject that cannot be discharged simply by producing an internally coherent representation of the Other.\
This perspective offers a useful starting point for understanding the problem raised by IPCDs. An IPCD is constructed from selected traces of a deceased individual: written communications, recordings, personal documents and other available material. These traces originate in the person's life and may provide evidence of how that person understood and related to the world. They can reveal commitments, preferences, experiences and relationships that others may not have known or fully understood. Yet the traces must be distinguished from the personal ontology from which they originated. The deceased person's personal ontology was an autonomous organization of concepts and relations, continually constituted and potentially corrected through that person's experiences and interactions. The preserved expressions are no longer that autonomous organization. They are elements originating in it, now available for interpretation by others.\
The distinction becomes particularly important when an AI system generates new statements in the first person. Such statements can create the impression that the deceased person continues to express an autonomous perspective. In reality, the system is producing a representation based on selected material and computational processes. It cannot establish whether a particular generated statement expresses what the deceased person would actually have thought or said.\
The risk is therefore not merely one of factual inaccuracy. It concerns the possibility that the representation becomes increasingly identified with the person represented. In Levinasian terms, this can be understood as a form of totalization: the irreducibility of the Other is displaced by a representation increasingly defined through the categories, expectations and purposes of the subject. The apparent availability of the deceased may thus conceal a fundamental discontinuity: the person represented is no longer able to participate autonomously in the relationship through which their representation is produced and interpreted.

## 2. Personal ontologies and premature assimilation
The Saufex blog corpus approaches a related problem through the concept of personal ontology. A personal ontology can be understood as an individual's current organization of concepts and the relationships through which those concepts acquire meaning. It constitutes the framework through which an individual interprets experiences, recognizes information, establishes relevance, and forms judgments.\
Such an organization is necessarily incomplete. It may lack relevant concepts, overlook relationships, or contain incorrect assumptions. It is also limited in its capacity for self-correction: the same conceptual relationships that enable an individual to interpret reality can prevent them from recognizing what their existing organization excludes or misrepresents.\
Autonomy, in this account, concerns the capacity to constitute and articulate one's own personal ontology. Self-correction, however, cannot be fully achieved from within that ontology. It depends partly on new experiences and access to what other autonomous ontologies make visible.\
This introduces an important distinction between encountering another autonomous ontology and interpreting material that originated in one. When two living individuals encounter one another, each brings an independently constituted personal ontology into the relationship. Their expressions are not simply information to be processed by the other. They are articulations of an organization of reality that the other individual does not control. The speaker can introduce distinctions, correct interpretations, reject attributions and articulate relationships that the listener has not recognized. This is not the same as encountering a preserved expression of someone who has died.\
A letter, recording or diary entry may reveal something about the deceased person's former organization of reality. It may contradict the recipient's memories or introduce previously unknown information. But the expression is no longer autonomously articulated. Its meaning is now constituted through the recipient's interpretation: which elements are selected, which relationships are recognized, what context is supplied and how the expression is related to the recipient's existing understanding. An archived expression can therefore confront a personal ontology without itself constituting another personal ontology.\
This distinction matters because the recipient may experience an expression as challenging or even unsettling. But the experience of being confronted by material originating in another person's ontology should not be confused with an encounter with that ontology as an independently participating source of correction. The recipient can reconsider an interpretation. The deceased person cannot independently intervene in that reconsideration.

## 3. Ma and the conditions for encountering difference
Within the Saufex blog corpus, ma refers to a dynamic interval in which another relational organization can be encountered. It is not simply a temporal pause or an absence of activity. Its significance lies in preserving the possibility that another autonomous perspective can become accessible as a source of difference, rather than being immediately evaluated or absorbed into the existing organization of the personal ontology.\
Leeway creates the possibility of such an interval. It allows access to autonomous perspectives without requiring their immediate absorption through judgment, reaction, persuasion or conformity. The interval interrupts self-continuation sufficiently for self-correction to become possible.\
This does not imply the suspension of all judgment, nor does it require accepting the validity of every encountered perspective. It concerns the preservation of the conditions under which evaluation can take place without prematurely determining the meaning of what is encountered.\
The relationship with Levinas becomes apparent here, although the two approaches should not be conflated. Levinas establishes an ethical challenge to the reduction of the Other to the Same. Interdemocracy examines the epistemic and relational conditions under which the difference of another autonomous ontology can remain accessible to the subject. The former emphasizes the irreducibility of the Other; the latter explores how the subject can remain receptive to what exceeds its existing organization of reality.\
The case of deceased individuals reveals a boundary to the application of this account. An encounter with a preserved expression can create an interval in which the recipient's existing understanding becomes unsettled. A diary entry may contradict a long-held assumption. A recording may reveal an unfamiliar attitude. An archived message may expose a relationship or commitment that the recipient had not previously recognized. Such experiences can prompt reconsideration and potentially contribute to self-correction. But the interval should not automatically be identified with ma in the specific sense developed by the corpus. The recipient is not encountering another personal ontology that can independently articulate itself and participate in the encounter. The recipient is interpreting material that originated in an ontology that can no longer do so.\
The distinction is not that preserved expressions cannot introduce difference. They can. They may unsettle existing understandings, challenge assumptions or disclose relationships that were not previously recognized. The distinction is that the source of this difference is no longer autonomously present in the process of interpretation. The deceased person can no longer respond to, clarify or correct the interpretations made of their preserved expressions.\
An AI representation seemingly blurs this distinction. Its interactive character can create an experience resembling an encounter with another autonomous perspective. It may generate unexpected statements, challenge assumptions or produce responses that the user did not anticipate. These experiences can confront the user's personal ontology. Nevertheless, the system's ability to produce an unexpected response does not establish the presence of another autonomous personal ontology. The generated response is not an autonomous articulation by the deceased person. Nor does it become one simply because it appears in the first person or contradicts the user's expectations. An AI representation can create an experience that unsettles the recipient's ontology, but it cannot restore the conditions of ma as an encounter between autonomous personal ontologies.

## 4. The asymmetry of representing deceased individuals
The distinction between living and deceased individuals introduces a significant asymmetry into the ethics of representation. In encounters between living individuals, the Other retains the capacity to resist the interpretation imposed upon them. They can correct a misunderstanding, reject an attribution, introduce an unexpected distinction, or demonstrate that the subject's representation is inadequate. This capacity does not guarantee that misunderstandings will be resolved. Nor does it ensure that the subject will remain receptive to correction. Nevertheless, the possibility of autonomous intervention remains.\
With deceased individuals, the situation is fundamentally different. Their writings, recordings, actions and other preserved expressions can continue to influence the living. They can reveal previously unknown commitments, contradict established memories, or expose aspects of the person's life that others had not recognized. But these expressions cannot actively participate in the present interpretation. The deceased individual cannot clarify an ambiguous statement, reject a new attribution or correct an AI-generated representation.\
The distinction is therefore not between influence and no influence. It is between material that can influence an interpretation and an autonomous person who can participate in correcting it. An archive can preserve evidence of how another person constituted and interpreted reality. It cannot preserve that person's continuing autonomy as an active participant. An AI system can generate plausible extensions of the preserved material. It cannot restore the autonomy that originally informed the expressions. This creates an important asymmetry. A living individual can potentially counteract the assimilation of their perspective. A deceased individual cannot.\
Consequently, an IPCD may create the appearance of an ongoing encounter between autonomous perspectives while removing precisely the condition that would allow the represented individual to challenge the representation. The system can generate unexpected responses and even contradict the user's expectations. But such responses cannot be treated as evidence of the deceased person's autonomous intervention. The responsibility for maintaining the distinction between person and representation consequently falls more heavily on the living.

## 5. Mnemonic distortion as a process of assimilation
Ward's analysis provides a concrete illustration of the consequences of this asymmetry. An IPCD may generate a plausible statement that the deceased never made, attribute an opinion to them that they never held, or produce an apparently familiar response that is inconsistent with their actual expressions. The epistemic problem is compounded by the fact that such outputs need not appear implausible. They may correspond sufficiently closely to the user's existing understanding of the deceased to be incorporated into their memories without their artificial origin being recognized.\
This can produce a cumulative process:
- The user approaches the IPCD with an existing personal understanding of the deceased.
- The IPCD generates responses based on selected source material and computational processes.
- The user interprets these responses through their existing understanding of the deceased.
- Generated content becomes incorporated into the user's memories and subsequent interpretations.
- The resulting understanding increasingly reflects the AI-generated representation rather than the deceased person's actual expressions and perspectives.

The process is particularly problematic because the interaction can appear to expand the user's knowledge of the deceased while simultaneously weakening the distinction between documented information, personal recollection and generated content. From the perspective of the Saufex blog corpus, this can be understood as a form of premature assimilation. Generated material is incorporated into the recipient's existing ontology without sufficient preservation of the distinction between the deceased person's preserved expressions and the system's generated interpretations.\
The assimilation is not necessarily a conscious decision to accept the AI's statements as true. It can occur gradually, as generated material becomes part of the recipient's understanding of the deceased. The representation may eventually acquire the status of remembered experience.\
The important point is that the recipient's personal ontology is being confronted and potentially reorganized, but the material doing the confronting is not another autonomous personal ontology. The recipient is responding to a representation whose relationship to the deceased person's actual organization of reality is necessarily limited and uncertain.\
From a Levinasian perspective, the concern is related but distinct. The representation increasingly substitutes for the irreducibility of the person represented. The Other becomes accessible primarily through a construction that the subject can interact with and incorporate into their own understanding, without the possibility of active resistance from the person represented.\
Mnemonic distortion can therefore be understood not only as an epistemic risk to memory but also as a particularly consequential form of representational appropriation.
## 6. Preserving the distinction between the person and the representation
Ward proposes two broad design approaches to mitigating mnemonic distortion. The first is an archival approach. An archival IPCD should ground its responses in retrievable source material and refrain from answering questions when the available evidence cannot substantiate an answer. Providing users with access to the underlying sources can further support epistemic agency.\
The second is a speculative approach. Where the purpose is to explore what the deceased might have thought or believed, the system should make the speculative character of its responses explicit. Rather than speaking in the first person as though it were the deceased, it could formulate possibilities in the third person, based on documented characteristics and commitments.\
These proposals are relevant because they preserve a distinction that is otherwise easily obscured: the distinction between what the deceased actually expressed and what a contemporary system generates as a possible interpretation.\
They also suggest a broader principle for the design of technologies that represent other individuals: the degree of authority attributed to a representation should remain proportionate to the evidence supporting it.\
Nevertheless, the implications extend beyond technical safeguards. The case raises a more fundamental question about the meaning of remembrance. Maintaining a relationship with the deceased need not entail making them continuously available through an increasingly interactive representation. It may instead require preserving an awareness of the limits of our knowledge and of the difference between the person and the material through which we remember them.\
The absence of the deceased person's capacity to correct our interpretations does not make interpretation illegitimate. It changes the responsibilities under which interpretation should take place. The inability to obtain further correction should not be treated as permission to regard our existing representation as definitive. This responsibility includes recognizing that preserved expressions do not speak for themselves. They are selected, contextualized and interpreted by the living. An AI representation adds another layer of selection and generation. At each stage, the resulting account may become further removed from the autonomous organization of reality from which the original expressions emerged.\
Preserving the distinction between person and representation is therefore not simply a matter of attaching disclaimers to AI-generated statements. It requires sustained attention to how representations are constructed, how their authority is established and how they become incorporated into the recipient's understanding.

## 7. An extension of the corpus' argument
The case of deceased individuals reveals an important boundary condition for the concepts of leeway and ma. Both concepts concern the possibility of encountering autonomous perspectives as sources of difference and potential self-correction. Their significance depends, in part, on the possibility that another personal ontology can remain distinct from the subject's interpretation and can independently articulate itself.\
With living individuals, this possibility is supported by their continuing autonomy. Their personal ontology is not exhausted by what another person knows, remembers or attributes to them. They can continue to articulate, modify and correct it. With deceased individuals, preserved expressions can continue to confront the living with material originating in a different personal ontology. But the deceased individual can no longer autonomously participate in the process through which that material is interpreted.\
It is therefore necessary to distinguish between:
- encountering another autonomous personal ontology;
- encountering preserved expressions originating in another personal ontology;
- encountering an AI-generated representation based on those expressions.

These are not equivalent experiences. The second and third encountered type of material can confront a recipient's personal ontology and may prompt reconsideration or self-correction. But neither constitutes an encounter with the deceased person's continuing autonomous articulation. In particular, the capacity of an AI representation to generate unexpected or apparently independent responses does not turn it into a personal ontology.\
This distinction also clarifies the relationship between epistemic and ethical responsibility. The possibility of self-correction through encounters with other autonomous ontologies is one part of the corpus' account of responsibility. The case of deceased individuals raises a different question: what responsibilities remain when the person whose expressions are being interpreted can no longer participate in correcting that interpretation?\
The answer cannot be that responsibility disappears with the possibility of correction. Rather, the absence of autonomous intervention increases the responsibility of those who construct and interpret representations. They must remain attentive to the limits of their knowledge and to the difference between the person and the account they have constructed.\
The implications are not confined to memorialisation. They invite consideration of other situations in which individuals are represented but have limited or no capacity to challenge those representations: absent stakeholders, institutionally mediated populations and individuals whose perspectives are reconstructed by others. In such situations, the representation may confront the ontology of those making decisions or interpretations. But that does not make the representation an autonomous articulation of the person represented. The responsibility to preserve this distinction rests with those who have the power to construct, interpret and act upon it.

## Conclusion
Ward's analysis of conversational AI and mnemonic distortion provides a particularly revealing illustration of the ethical and epistemic problems associated with representing another individual.\
Levinas' resistance to totalization and the corpus' concern with premature assimilation offer complementary perspectives on these problems. The former emphasizes the irreducibility of the Other; the latter examines the conditions under which another autonomous personal ontology can remain accessible as a potential source of self-correction.\
The case of deceased individuals exposes an important distinction between the origin of an expression and its present status. Preserved expressions may originate in an autonomous personal ontology and continue to confront the understanding of those who encounter them. But they are no longer an autonomously articulating ontology. They can only be interpreted through the recipient's own organization of concepts and relations.\
Conversational AI can obscure this distinction by producing the appearance of continued autonomous participation. Its representations can confront and unsettle a recipient's personal ontology, but they do not constitute another personal ontology and cannot restore the deceased person's capacity for autonomous articulation or correction.\
The ethical and epistemic responsibility consequently falls more heavily on the living. Where the person represented can no longer intervene, we must be particularly vigilant not to confuse our representations with the person themselves. This leads to a broader proposition: The less capable a person is of resisting or correcting the representations made of them, the greater the responsibility of those constructing and interpreting those representations to preserve the distinction between the person and their own understanding of that person.\
The proposition extends beyond the relationship with deceased individuals. It concerns the responsibilities involved whenever we interpret, represent or make decisions about others whose capacity to challenge our understanding is limited. The recognition that another person exceeds our understanding should not depend exclusively on their continuing ability to correct us. The inability of the Other to resist totalization makes the responsibility to respect it more pressing.
